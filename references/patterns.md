# Pattern catalogue (Phase 5)

## How to use this list

- It is a diagnostic list. A pattern is a reason to look closely, not proof and not a rule; careful writers use each of these on purpose sometimes.
- **Strong**: act on one clear instance. **Weak**: act only when it clusters with other tells in the same passage.
- Fix the sentence's job, not its words: state the claim directly, make it specific from the material, merge, or cut. A synonym swap leaves the pattern in place and often creates an odd collocation.
- Never add a fact to fix a pattern. If the specific version needs a detail you do not have, ask, or write a plainer sentence.
- The brief and the register profile override this list. Each entry says when to leave the pattern alone.
- After revising, run section G (overcorrection) on your own changes.

## A. Content and stance

Expert readers notice these first, and they survive any amount of word-level editing.

### A1. Interchangeable sentence (strong)
- **Watch for:** a sentence that would fit unchanged into any text on the topic. "Effective communication is essential in today's workplace." "This has significant implications for policymakers."
- **Why:** it carries nothing from this writer's material. Expert annotators single out "safe", unsurprising content; slop studies point to low information density and relevance.
- **Fix:** replace it with the specific claim the material supports (who, what, how much, compared with what), or cut it.
- **Leave it when:** the genre needs a one-line orientation for non-specialists and the sentence is short.

### A2. Inflated significance (strong)
- **Watch for:** plays a pivotal or crucial role, marks a turning point, a testament to, underscores the importance of, reflects a broader trend, paves the way for, far-reaching implications; "Despite these challenges, X continues to…".
- **Why:** an ordinary fact is dressed as momentous, and the claim of significance has no support.
- **Fix:** keep the fact. If its significance matters, state that as a separate claim with its reason and evidence.
- **Leave it when:** the significance is the user's argued point and the text supports it.

### A3. Vague or borrowed authority (strong)
- **Watch for:** experts argue, research shows, studies suggest, scholars have noted, it is widely recognised, critics contend, with no citation; lists of prestigious outlets.
- **Why:** an unnamed authority stands in for a source. An academic reader checks and finds nothing.
- **Fix:** name the source from the material and what it found. Otherwise cut the claim or mark `[NEED: source]`.
- **Leave it when:** a citation follows and the attribution is accurate.

### A4. Missing authorial stance (strong in argumentative and academic genres)
- **Watch for:** a survey of views with no position; "some argue… others argue…" left unresolved; no self-mention where the genre allows it; a conclusion that summarises instead of concluding.
- **Why:** corpus studies find ChatGPT essays use significantly fewer stance expressions, fewer hedges and boosters, and show weaker authorial presence than student essays; they also use fewer epistemic markers.
- **Fix:** state the position from the brief, with its reason and the confidence the evidence supports. Use "I" or "we" as the profile allows.
- **Leave it when:** the genre is deliberately neutral (literature review sections, reference text, minutes).

### A5. Miscalibrated certainty (strong when systematic)
- **Watch for:** stacked hedges (may potentially, could possibly suggest, it could be argued that… might); boosters on everything (clearly, significantly, undoubtedly, crucially); or speculative claims stated as fact.
- **Why:** certainty should track the evidence. Uniform hedging or boosting shows the claims were not weighed.
- **Fix:** one hedge where the evidence is uncertain, with the reason if useful ("with a sample of 40, …"); none on reported facts; boosters only where the evidence is strong and the point contested. Check each claim's strength against the Phase 2 inventory.
- **Leave it when:** the field requires a formula ("the results suggest").

### A6. Over-explaining, or writing for the wrong reader (strong)
- **Watch for:** definitions of terms the reader knows; background the reader supplied; the main point arriving last; a restatement of the question before the answer.
- **Why:** the text is written for a reader with no context. Expert annotators cite over-explanation and irrelevant detail.
- **Fix:** cut what the reader in the brief already knows, and lead with the point.
- **Leave it when:** the brief names a mixed or lay audience.

### A7. Arguing with no one; not X but Y (strong)
- **Watch for:** it's not just X, it's Y; not merely X but Y; this isn't about X; this does not mean X. It means Y; X rather than Y; an objection or option that nobody raised.
- **Why:** the negative half invents a position so the positive half looks larger.
- **Fix:** state Y. Keep the contrast only when X is a view the reader actually holds or a cited source states, or when both halves carry information.
- **Leave it when:** X is attributed and answered.

### A8. Aphorisms and deep-sounding frames (strong)
- **Watch for:** at its core, the real question is, fundamentally, the heart of the matter, X is the currency of Y, X is not a tool but a mirror.
- **Why:** an ordinary point is presented as a hidden truth.
- **Fix:** state the specific claim.
- **Leave it when:** it is a quotation.

## B. Discourse and structure

### B1. Template paragraph (weak; strong when most paragraphs share it)
- **Watch for:** topic sentence, two or three supporting sentences, then a closing sentence restating the paragraph's significance; paragraphs of similar length throughout.
- **Why:** structure by default, not by argument. The closing line adds nothing.
- **Fix:** end on the last substantive sentence; let paragraph length follow how much each point needs.
- **Leave it when:** the closing sentence draws a new inference that the next paragraph builds on.

### B2. Template document (strong)
- **Watch for:** an introduction that restates the prompt; three symmetrical body sections; a "challenges" section followed by optimism; a conclusion opening "In conclusion" that summarises everything; equal weight for unequal points.
- **Why:** the outline came from a default rather than from the argument.
- **Fix:** rebuild the outline from the inventory (Phase 2); weight sections by importance; end on the conclusion the genre asks for.
- **Leave it when:** the venue mandates the structure (IMRaD, rubric headings). A research article's limitations section is required, not a tell.

### B3. One-line closers and dramatic fragments (strong)
- **Watch for:** one-sentence paragraphs that restate; "That matters." "And that changes everything." "The result? …"; rows of fragments.
- **Why:** emphasis without content.
- **Fix:** cut, or merge into a sentence that carries a new fact.

### B4. Run-ups and self-narration (strong; weak in long academic texts)
- **Watch for:** Let's explore; It is important to note that; It is worth mentioning that; This section will discuss; In this essay I will…; "the table below shows" when it is obvious; accounts of how the text was produced.
- **Why:** the text talks about itself instead of making its point.
- **Fix:** make the point.
- **Leave it when:** a long document needs a short roadmap that the genre expects (thesis chapter openings, "Section 3 describes the data"), given once.

### B5. Connector-led cohesion (weak)
- **Watch for:** many sentences opening with Moreover, Furthermore, Additionally, In addition, Notably, Importantly, Ultimately.
- **Why:** cohesion is carried by markers rather than by content. ChatGPT essays lean on transitional and structuring bundles.
- **Fix:** drop the marker when the relation is plain; link by repeating the key term or referring back ("This fall in margins…"); keep connectives that mark a real contrast, cause or concession.

### B6. Elegant variation (strong in technical and academic text)
- **Watch for:** one referent named by rotating synonyms (the firm, the company, the organisation, the entity; the model, the framework, the approach).
- **Why:** it reads as avoiding repetition rather than referring, and a careful reader wonders whether these are different things.
- **Fix:** one term per concept; use pronouns and "this + noun" where the reference is clear.

### B7. Information-flow breaks (weak)
- **Watch for:** sentences that open with long, new information and end with old; consecutive sentences whose subjects jump between topics.
- **Why:** the reader cannot see how each sentence connects to the last. Long nominal subjects are common in generated prose.
- **Fix:** old information first, new information last; keep a consistent topic across the paragraph.

### B8. Equal treatment of unequal points (weak)
- **Watch for:** every point given the same length; "on the one hand… on the other" where the evidence is not balanced.
- **Fix:** give space by importance and say which side the evidence favours.

## C. Syntax

### C1. Trailing -ing commentary (strong)
- **Watch for:** a participle clause added to the end of a sentence to comment on it: ", highlighting…", ", underscoring…", ", reflecting…", ", contributing to…", ", ensuring…", ", demonstrating…", ", paving the way for…".
- **Why:** instruction-tuned models use present participial clauses at two to five times the human rate (GPT-4o: 5.3 times). The clause's logical subject is vague and its claim is usually unsupported.
- **Before:** Interest rates rose by 75 basis points in 2023, highlighting the central bank's commitment to price stability.
- **After:** The central bank raised rates by 75 basis points in 2023. (If the motive matters and the material supports it: "It said the increase was needed to return inflation to target.")
- **Leave it when:** the clause gives a concrete, supported fact about manner or simultaneous action: "We surveyed 400 firms, using the 2022 register as the sampling frame."

### C2. Nominal chains (weak; strong in clusters)
- **Watch for:** the implementation of, the utilisation of, the enhancement of, the facilitation of; chains such as "the optimisation of the allocation of resources".
- **Why:** GPT-4o uses nominalisations at about twice the human rate, and noun-based bundles dominate ChatGPT essays. Abstract nouns hide who does what.
- **Fix:** name the actor and use the verb: "firms allocate resources more efficiently".
- **Leave it when:** the noun is a technical term (inflation targeting) or the genre uses a nominal style for concision (titles, abstracts, legal text).

### C3. Pairs and triads by default (weak; strong in clusters)
- **Watch for:** binomials (clear and concise, robust and scalable, challenges and opportunities); triads (efficient, scalable and sustainable; three parallel examples; three short facts and then a lesson).
- **Why:** GPT-4o uses phrasal coordination at about 1.9 times the human rate, and expert readers cite "lists of three".
- **Fix:** keep only the items the material supports and that add distinct information. One precise word beats two vague ones.
- **Leave it when:** there really are two or three distinct things.

### C4. Avoiding "is" and "has" (weak)
- **Watch for:** serves as, stands as, functions as, represents, boasts, features, offers (meaning "has").
- **Fix:** is, are, has.
- **Leave it when:** the longer verb is more precise ("the index represents the median household").

### C5. Uniform sentence shape (weak)
- **Watch for:** runs of sentences built the same way (introductory phrase, main clause, trailing modifier), of similar length, or with the same opening (The…, This…).
- **Why:** models reuse part-of-speech templates at a higher rate than human writers.
- **Fix:** rewrite from the content: a short sentence for the key finding, closely related claims combined, each sentence opening with its topic. Do not vary mechanically.

### C6. Voice that does not fit the genre (weak)
- **Watch for:** procedures rewritten into actor-heavy active ("We then carefully examined…") where the field writes passive; or a persuasive genre full of agentless passives that hide responsibility.
- **Why:** generated prose uses fewer agentless passives than human writing (about half the human rate for GPT-4o), so converting every passive to active can make academic text less natural, not more.
- **Fix:** follow the register profile and the information flow.

### C7. Dashes as an all-purpose connector (weak)
- **Watch for:** frequent em dashes joining clauses or setting off asides.
- **Fix:** choose the relation: comma, colon, semicolon, parentheses or a new sentence. If the sample uses dashes, match its rate.
- **Leave it when:** the style guide or sample uses them. Occasional dashes are normal in edited prose.

## D. Lexis

All weak alone. The word usually sits inside a pattern from A to C; fix that pattern and the word goes with it.

### D1. Over-represented vocabulary in clusters (weak)
- **Watch for clusters of:** delve, underscore, showcase, highlight (verb), pivotal, crucial, key (adjective), intricate, nuanced, multifaceted, comprehensive, robust (figurative), seamless, landscape (figurative), realm, tapestry, testament, foster, leverage, navigate (figurative), harness, bolster, garner, enhance, align with, meticulous, commendable, invaluable, vibrant, profound; "in today's rapidly evolving…".
- **Why:** the frequency of words such as "delves", "showcasing" and "underscores" in scientific abstracts jumped after LLMs appeared, and preference training is a likely cause. Expert readers' false alarms also cite vocabulary, because people use these words too.
- **Fix:** fix the padding around the word; never swap one word for another.
- **Leave it when:** the word is the precise term (robust standard errors) or appears once in otherwise specific prose.

### D2. Predictable bundles (weak)
- **Watch for:** plays a crucial role in, a wide range of, in terms of, shed light on, pave the way for, a deeper understanding of, in the context of.
- **Fix:** state the specific relation or quantity ("accounts for 40% of", "explains why").

### D3. Promotional register (strong in neutral genres)
- **Watch for:** boasts, nestled, vibrant, world-class, cutting-edge, groundbreaking, renowned, rich heritage, state-of-the-art, unparalleled.
- **Fix:** say what the thing is or does, with numbers if the material has them.
- **Leave it when:** the genre is marketing copy.

### D4. Vague relation words (weak)
- **Watch for:** associated with, linked to, in connection with, tied to, when the material gives the actual relation.
- **Fix:** name the relation (caused, preceded, correlated with, founded, chaired). If the material does not say, keep the vague wording rather than invent one.

## E. Formatting

### E1. Bold labels and decorative bold (strong in prose genres)
- **Watch for:** list items that each open with a bold label and a colon; bold for emphasis in running text.
- **Fix:** prose, or plain list items if the genre uses lists.

### E2. Lists where an argument belongs (strong in essays and papers)
- **Watch for:** reasoning broken into bullets, losing the "because" and "therefore" links.
- **Fix:** write the reasoning as connected prose.
- **Leave it when:** the genre uses lists (business reports, recommendations, procedures).

### E3. Heading decoration (weak)
- **Watch for:** emoji; title case where the style guide uses sentence case; a heading followed by a sentence that restates it.
- **Leave it when:** the style guide prescribes title case (APA 7 headings, for example).

## F. Residue (strong; remove)

### F1. Chat wrapper
"Certainly!", "Great question", "I hope this helps", "Let me know if…", "Here is a revised version" inside the deliverable.

### F2. Knowledge-limit disclaimers and guesses
"As of my last update", "while specific details are limited", "likely", "it is believed that". Replace with the fact from the material, or cut.

## G. Overcorrection

Check your own revisions for these. Expert readers notice them quickly; they are the signature of "humanised" text.

- **G1. Fake informality:** contractions, slang, rhetorical questions, "Honestly,", "Here's the thing" in formal genres. Recent models use contractions too, so informality is no evidence of a human writer.
- **G2. Mechanical rhythm:** short sentences or one-line paragraphs added for variety.
- **G3. Thesaurus substitution:** a common, precise word replaced by a rarer one; odd collocations ("harness insights", "bolster understanding"); edited passages in a different register from unedited ones.
- **G4. Invented colour or imperfection:** errors, hesitations, anecdotes or opinions the user did not give.
- **G5. Over-deletion:** removing what the genre requires (limitations, method detail, required signposting, a polite opening in an email), every hedge, or every transition.
- **G6. Meaning drift:** a claim made stronger or weaker, a qualification lost, a number or name changed. Compare with the Phase 2 inventory.
