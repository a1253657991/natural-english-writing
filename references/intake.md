# Intake: questions and brief template

Use this in Phase 1, after reading the user's materials. Skip any question that the materials or the conversation already answer. Ask the rounds in order of impact: task and reader first, conventions last. Ask in the user's language. For each question, give a short reason and a recommended default where a sensible one exists.

There is no limit on questions. The stopping rule is: every field of the brief is either answered or marked `[ASSUMED]` with the user's agreement, and nothing in the material is ambiguous.

## Round 1: task, genre, reader, purpose

1. **Task mode.** Write from notes, revise your English draft, turn a Chinese (or other) draft into English, or audit only? *Why: decides how much of the wording may change.*
2. **Genre and venue.** What is the document, and where does it go (journal and section, course and assessment, employer and role, client)? Is there a template, author guideline, rubric, prompt or call you can share? *Why: the genre fixes structure and register.*
3. **Reader.** Who reads it first, and who decides? Their field and expertise, what they already know, and how they will read: skim, assess against criteria, or scrutinise the language. If linguists or language specialists are reading, are they assessing content, language, or both? *Why: decides what to explain and what to leave out.*
4. **Purpose.** After reading, what should the reader believe, decide or do? What is the single main point? *Why: everything else is ordered around it.*
5. **Length and structure.** Word or page limit (do references count?), and any required sections or headings.

## Round 2: content

6. **Material.** Paste your notes, data, sources and earlier versions. Which points are fixed, which can be cut, and is anything confidential?
7. **Evidence.** For each main claim, what supports it (data, citation, your experience)? Which claims are established and which are your interpretation? *Why: sets how strongly each claim can be stated.*
8. **Stance.** Where do you stand on the main question, and how firmly? Any point to concede or limitation to state?
9. **Terminology.** Field terms, abbreviations and names to use or avoid; preferred English for Chinese terms.

## Round 3: voice and conventions

10. **Author.** Who is speaking: you, a team, an organisation? I, we, or impersonal? Your position relative to the reader?
11. **Samples.** Your own English writing that you are happy with, and/or a model text from the target venue. *Why: a sample is the most reliable register target.*
12. **English variety.** Australian, British or American spelling and usage? *Default: match the venue; Australian institutions usually expect Australian English.*
13. **Style guide.** Citation style (APA 7, Harvard, Chicago, venue style), number and date formats, heading style.
14. **Formatting.** Prose only, or are headings, lists and tables acceptable?
15. **Register preferences.** How formal? First person allowed? Contractions (*default: none in formal writing*)? Dashes? Anything you disliked in earlier AI output?

## Round 4: delivery and constraints

16. **Deliverable.** Plain text in the chat, Markdown, or a Word file? Clean copy or visible changes? How much explanation, and in which language?
17. **Constraints.** Deadline; the venue's rules on AI assistance or disclosure; anything that must not change (quotations, legal wording, figures).

## Ambiguity checks on the material

Ask whenever you find:

- numbers or names that conflict between sources;
- a pronoun or "this" whose referent is unclear;
- an undefined abbreviation, or a term used in two senses;
- a claim of unclear strength (is "X causes Y" a finding, a hypothesis or a view?);
- a point that seems to contradict the stated purpose or reader;
- a source cited without enough detail to cite it properly.
